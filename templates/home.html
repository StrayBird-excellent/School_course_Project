What is breast cancer

Cancers are usually named for the tissue where they start. Breast cancer begins in breast tissue. Like other cancers, it can grow into nearby tissue and spread to other parts of the body (metastasis).

Who is affected by breast cancer

Breast cancer is a leading cause of cancer death in women (after lung cancer). About 1 in 8 women may develop invasive breast cancer in her lifetime. Screening and early detection remain important public health tools.

Risk Factors

  • Gender: Women are much more likely to develop breast cancer than men.
  • Age: Risk increases with age, especially after 50.
  • Family history: Having a close relative with breast cancer increases risk.
  • Genetic mutations: BRCA1 and BRCA2 gene mutations increase risk.
  • Hormone replacement therapy: Long-term use may increase risk.
  • Obesity: Being overweight or obese increases risk, especially after menopause.
  • Alcohol consumption: Drinking alcohol increases risk.

Prevention Tips

  • Maintain a healthy weight
  • Exercise regularly (at least 150 minutes per week)
  • Limit alcohol consumption
  • Breastfeed if possible
  • Get regular screening mammograms
  • Know your family history

Statistics

According to the American Cancer Society:

  • About 1 in 8 women will develop invasive breast cancer during their lifetime
  • About 1 in 833 men will develop breast cancer during their lifetime
  • Early detection through screening can significantly improve survival rates

Common symptoms of breast cancer

Breast cancer can present with various symptoms. It's important to be aware of any changes in your breasts and consult a healthcare provider if you notice anything unusual.

Common Symptoms

  • A new lump or mass: Often painless, hard, and has irregular edges, but can also be soft, round, tender, or even painful.
  • Change in breast size or shape: One breast may become larger or lower than the other.
  • Nipple changes: Nipple discharge (other than breast milk), nipple retraction (turning inward), or nipple skin that is scaly, crusty, or flaking.
  • Skin changes: Dimpling, puckering, or redness of the breast skin, similar to the skin of an orange (peau d'orange).
  • Breast or nipple pain: While most breast cancers are painless, some can cause pain.
  • Swelling: Swelling of all or part of a breast (even if no lump is felt).
  • Lymph node changes: Swelling of lymph nodes under the arm or around the collarbone.

When to See a Doctor

You should consult a healthcare provider if you notice any of the following:

  • A new lump or mass in your breast or underarm
  • Any change in the size, shape, or appearance of your breast
  • Any discharge from your nipple
  • Persistent breast pain
  • Redness, scaliness, or thickening of the breast skin or nipple
  • Nipple retraction or inversion

Importance of Early Detection

Early detection of breast cancer greatly improves treatment outcomes. Regular self-exams, clinical breast exams, and mammograms are important screening tools. The American Cancer Society recommends:

  • Women aged 40-44 should have the choice to start annual mammograms
  • Women aged 45-54 should get annual mammograms
  • Women aged 55+ should switch to mammograms every 2 years or continue annual screening
